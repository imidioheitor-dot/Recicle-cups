01 — Material

What is
actually
in the bin

Notre Dame Stadium collects empty water bottles only in its green barrels. That single policy is a gift: it hands us a feedstock stream that is already 70 % sorted before we touch it.

The bottle is not one plastic. The body is PET #1, the cap is almost always PP #5, and the ring left behind is PP or HDPE. Those three polymers melt at different temperatures and do not blend. Separating them is not optional housekeeping — it is the whole engineering problem.

1
PET
Polyethylene terephthalate

The bottle body. Over 70 % of US beverage bottles. Strong, optically clear, and the single largest thing we can harvest. It is also hygroscopic — it drinks water out of the air, and water is what destroys it in the melt.

HDPE
High-density polyethylene

Milk jugs, detergent bottles, some caps. Chemically tough and pleasant to shred. Its flaw is thermal: it shrinks hard on cooling, so printed parts warp off the bed unless the geometry stays low and wide.

Polypropylene

Every cap, every tamper ring. Individually small, collectively enormous — and the number-one contaminant risk. A few grams of PP inside a PET melt is enough to make the entire batch of filament brittle and unusable.

PVC

Reject on sight

The finding that shapes everything

PET does not fail because it is weak. It fails because it is wet. Moisture trapped in the flake causes hydrolysis in the melt: water molecules cut the polymer chains, and what comes out of the nozzle is brittle enough to snap in your fingers.

This is why our build spends real money on a dehydrator before it spends anything on a fancy screw. Four hours at 65 °C is the difference between filament and garbage.

02 — Machines

What Notre Dame
already
owns

We do not need to buy a printer. The campus has several — but only some of them will accept a filament we made ourselves.

This distinction decides the project. Industrial machines from Stratasys read a chip on the material cartridge and refuse anything unrecognised. Open-material machines in the Prusa family will happily print whatever you feed them. Our filament only has a home on the open ones.

03 — Build

Two lines,
not one

A commercial desktop extruder — the Filabot EX2 — sells for US$ 2,945. That is three times our entire budget, and it settles the architecture question immediately: we build, we do not buy.

Trying to shred and screw-extrude everything on one machine would blow the ceiling. So the line splits by what each polymer is actually good at.

Line A — Pultrusion
PET bottle → continuous strip → filament
PET #1

The bottle is never shredded. A jig slices it into one unbroken ribbon, which is drawn through a heated nozzle and re-formed as round 1.75 mm filament. No screw, no auger, no melt reservoir — which is exactly why it fits the budget.

04 — Risk

What kills
this project

Three failure modes account for nearly every abandoned recycled-filament build we found in the literature. Each one has a specific, budgeted countermeasure in the bill of materials above.

Severity: fatal
Moisture in the melt

PET is hygroscopic. Any water still in the flake hydrolyses at extrusion temperature, cleaving polymer chains. The filament looks fine and snaps under its own weight.

Countermeasure4 h at 65 °C in the dehydrator, immediately before every run. Filament stored with desiccant in a sealed box. $45, line item one.
Severity: fatal
Cross-polymer contamination

A few grams of PP from a single missed cap will make an entire PET batch brittle. The two polymers do not blend; they form weak boundaries that fracture.

CountermeasurePhysical separation at intake, not sorting later. Caps and rings go to Line B by hand before a bottle ever reaches the cutter. Two bins, one rule.
Severity: high
Diameter drift

Filament outside 1.75 ± 0.05 mm jams extruders or under-extrudes. Pultrusion drift comes from inconsistent pull speed and strip width.

CountermeasureFixed-width cutter jig plus closed-loop pull rate. Every spool is sampled at ten points on the Engineering Innovation Hub metrology bench before it is cleared for printing.

05 — Sequence

Eight weeks
to first spool

Ordered so that the cheapest, highest-information experiments happen first. If pultrusion fails in week three, we have spent $342 and learned it — not $968.

Week 01—02
Intake & characterisation

Collect one game's worth of bottles. Sort, weigh, and log by polymer. Establish the real ratio of PET body to PP cap mass — every downstream decision depends on that number being measured, not assumed.

Week 03—04
Line A — pultrusion

Assemble the ReCreator kit. Dial in nozzle temperature and pull rate against diameter. Target: 200 g of filament holding 1.75 ± 0.05 mm across a full spool.

Week 05—06
Line B — shredder

Laser-cut the frame at the Innovation Lab, stack the blade set, wire the reducer and E-stop. First cap flake, first pressed coaster. Safety sign-off before the motor turns under load.

Week 07
Print validation

Run our filament through an open-material Prusa. Benchmark tensile and layer adhesion against retail PETG. Publish the delta honestly — including where recycled loses.

Week 08
Product & tag

First dorm-need objects off the bed. NTAG215 tags encoded and seated into printed recesses. The loop closes: stadium bottle to labelled object in one building.
